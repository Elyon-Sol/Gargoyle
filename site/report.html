Technical report

Receipts and holds for agent tool calls

Verifiable authorization, human release, execution and evidence for the actions an AI assistant takes on a person's behalf

Justin LaPorte · Elyon-Sol · October 2026 · describes Gargoyle 0.15, wire format gargoyle/3, evidence format 4 · Apache-2.0

Abstract. AI assistants now act: they write files, create mail rules, send messages, and change records, through tools they call on a person's behalf. The products that govern those calls decide and log. None of them can later prove, to a party who trusts neither the vendor nor the workstation, what was authorized, by whom, and what ran. This work presents Gargoyle, a small gate for the Model Context Protocol that turns every tool call into a signed, single-use receipt bound to its arguments and to a policy version; holds the calls that matter until a named person signs a release with a key no server holds; signs what ran, with the target's state before and after; records every decision, refusals included, in a hash-chained evidence log; and keeps an issuer-signed witness on a separate host so the workstation's own record can be checked against an independent one. The chain verifies offline, in Python and TypeScript, against published vectors in RFC 8785 canonical form. It is evaluated on a live deployment with directory identities for the person asking and the person approving: forty-five verified rows covering free, held and refused calls, a state-bound write, an exact undo and one the audit declined to call exact, a scoped plan, a reconciliation, and a hand edit caught around the gate. The threat model is stated plainly: on a workstation its user administers, the gate constrains the assistant, not the user, and circumvention is detected rather than prevented. The work is a prerelease by one author, proven in one deployment, and its remaining limits are listed.

1. Introduction: the claim nobody makes

An assistant that can only talk is governed by what it says. An assistant that can act is governed by what it does, and in 2026 assistants act through tools: a filesystem, a mailbox, a calendar, a database, a chat channel, reached through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) or a vendor's function-calling interface. The controls that have grown up around this are of three kinds. Gateways sit in the path, evaluate a policy, pause a call for a human click, and log the outcome. Approval layers pause an agent and ask a person over Slack or email. Receipt protocols, in several Internet-Drafts and one prototype, sign a record of a decision or of an execution.

Each of these answers a question. None answers the question a client's auditor, a regulator, or a cautious managing engineer eventually asks: show me, without trusting your dashboard, your vendor, or the machine the assistant ran on, that this change was allowed by this policy, released by that person, and executed once as approved. The gateway's answer is a database row. The approval layer's answer is a resumed workflow. The receipt protocol's answer is one signed clause of a sentence that has several.

This work makes the whole sentence. For a tool call made by an assistant on a person's behalf, Gargoyle produces a chain of signed objects, each by a different key, such that anyone holding three public keys can verify offline that: a specific policy version authorized exactly this call for exactly this person; the named approvers the policy required released it, in the state of the target they were shown; exactly that call ran, once; the target went from this state to that state; and, if it was later undone, the undo was byte-exact. The chain is recorded in a hash-chained log on the workstation and reconciled against an independent signed record on another host, so the workstation's own account can be checked.

Three design commitments follow from making that sentence and run through everything below. First, nothing in the system asserts what its hashes cannot prove; the audit has twice declined to label something it could not verify, and both times it was right. Second, the gate is narrow on purpose: it is not a policy engine, not a gateway, not a governance platform, and it refuses breadth it would have to trust rather than verify. Third, limits are written into the specification rather than discovered by users; the most important of them, that on a user-administered workstation the gate constrains the assistant and not the user, is stated in section 9 and in the specification itself.

The rest of this document describes where the work came from (section 2), the model and its invariants (3), the specification and its conformance vectors (4), how real identities enter on both ends (5), the evidence system (6), the implementation (7), a live evaluation (8), the threat model and what it does not cover (9), the field it sits in (10), and what remains (11).

2. Lineage and prior work

2.1 Elyon-Sol, and what was kept

Gargoyle is the successor of Elyon-Sol, a deterministic, fail-closed HTTP admission gate developed through 2025 and 2026 and retired on 2026-07-20. Retirement meant the public nodes went offline and the project stopped being offered; the repository stayed open for a final review and fix pass on 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-08, and the retirement assessment and Zenodo revision 9, both dated 2026-10-08, are its closing record. Elyon-Sol derived its decisions from a formal admissibility specification it called the canon (v0.9.8.4 at retirement), issued Ed25519-signed, single-use admissibility envelopes, pinned the policy by SHA-256, ran a public break-it challenge with four hosted nodes, and published its evidence to Zenodo. Its test suite stood at 689 and its code at about thirty thousand lines across a gate, a sidecar, a ledger, a canon and a governance layer. Nobody broke it while the challenge ran. Nobody outside its author used it either.

The retirement assessment, written on 2026-10-08, put the problem in one sentence: drop the canon, drop the ledger, keep the receipt, and build the thing that answers a question somebody is asking. Four ideas survived into Gargoyle unchanged in substance: fail closed on every undecidable path; sign a single-use envelope bound to the exact action; pin the policy by hash so a changed policy invalidates what it decided; and publish the evidence rather than the promise. Everything else was left behind: the canon and its ledger, the governance wiring, the HTTP gate as the primary surface, the red-team apparatus as a product. Gargoyle began as sixteen modules and 4,200 lines lifted from Elyon-Sol's implementation directory, renamed, with their hard-coded paths into the canon removed; the new repository has its own history, license (Apache-2.0 rather than AGPL-3.0) and, since the move to RFC 8785, its own wire bytes. The two share an organization name and nothing else.

The lesson Elyon-Sol taught is not about cryptography. It is that a system which verifies itself for a year with no outside user has not been verified, and that a canon nobody asked for is a cost without a buyer. Gargoyle's evaluation (section 8) and its stated limits (section 9) exist because of that lesson.

2.2 Gateways and approval layers

MCP gateways with approval features became common in 2026. TrueFoundry, Monosign, hoop.dev, Akto and several smaller proxies hold a matched tools/call, create an approval request, notify a reviewer, and return the result with approval metadata; most keep an audit log, some a hash-chained one. They own breadth: tool discovery filtering, response inspection, masking, framework adapters, dashboards. Their approval is a state change inside a service the client must trust, and their audit is rows in a database that service controls. Approval layers such as withHuman and awaithumans, and the human-in-the-loop pattern in durable-workflow systems like Temporal, pause an agent, ask a person over a channel, and resume; they own the channels and the resumption semantics. Gargoyle differs from both in kind rather than degree: its approval is a signature verified at execution, and its evidence is verifiable by someone who trusts neither the gate's host nor the issuer's dashboard.

2.3 Receipt protocols

Three Internet-Drafts of 2026 each define one stage of the chain Gargoyle makes. Acta signed receipts (draft-farley-acta-signed-receipts) sign a machine-to-machine access decision for MCP tool calls with Ed25519 over RFC 8785 canonical JSON, verifiable with the issuer's key; they record a policy digest but no approval and no single-use semantics, and they must not carry tool arguments. EP authorization receipts (draft-schrock-ep-authorization-receipts, the EMILIA protocol) have a named human sign one exact high-risk action before execution with a key only they hold, with m-of-n signoffs, Merkle-anchored logs and a stated separation-of-duties rule; they record no execution and bind the human to a hash of the action rather than to a receipt. XAIP receipts (draft-xkumakichi-xaip-receipts) sign an execution record after a tool call, with hashes of inputs and outputs and an optional caller co-signature, under W3C DIDs; they carry no authorization and no policy. All three use RFC 8785, which is why Gargoyle moved to it (section 4.1). NOA Receipt, an Apache-2.0 prototype with an MCP proxy, signed hash-chained receipts, five verifier implementations and an adversarial corpus, is the closest running peer; its own documentation states that its approval proves intent, not execution, and that its record cannot say a side effect happened. Those are the clauses Gargoyle adds. Microsoft's Agent Governance Toolkit, in public preview, is the broadest neighbor: policy in YAML, OPA or Cedar, cryptographic agent identity, trust scores, hash-chained logs, SDKs in five languages; it decides and logs, and makes no verifiable claim about release or execution.

Section 10 returns to the comparison with a property-by-property table. The short version: each neighbor has one or two of Gargoyle's properties, none has the chain, and the convergence of several independent groups on the same shapes in the same year is the best evidence that the shapes are right.

3. The model

3.1 Parties

Five roles, each with its own key or none, and the design holds only if they stay separate.

PartyHoldsDoesTrusted for
Policyits configuration (a rules file, an OPA bundle)decides ELIGIBLE or REFUSE, and whether a human must releasethe decision; its pin is in every receipt
Issuerthe issuer keyasks the policy, signs the receipt, holds flagged calls, keeps the witnessthat receipts and the witness are its own; never the approval
Approvera key generated in their browser, never exportedreads the exact change and signs a grantthe release; never anything else
Executorthe executor key, on the workstationverifies receipt and grants, runs the call once, signs what ran, keeps the evidenceits own report, which the witness checks
Auditorthree public keysverifies everything offline, later, anywherenothing; that is the point

The issuer never holds an approver's private key and the executor never holds the issuer's. A grant signed with the issuer's key is refused by name (REF_APPROVAL_SOD) before its signature is even checked, so an issuer that approves its own holds produces nothing a verifier accepts.

3.2 Objects

The receipt is the issuer's signed statement of one decision. Its hashed region names the policy pin, the target, the tool, the SHA-256 of the canonical arguments, the principal, optionally the party the principal acts for, optionally the hash of the target's state, and the approval terms when the policy set a threshold or named approvers. Its signed region adds the expiry, the single-use decision id, and the approval request id. A receipt is honored only by an executor that re-derives every binding from the live call: the same tool, arguments that hash to the same value, the principal it authenticated, the policy pin it currently holds, and, when bound, the same state hash it computes now.

The grant is an approver's signed release of one receipt: the receipt's decision hash and hold id, a single-use grant id, an expiry, the approver's key id. A receipt whose terms require n approvers is released by n grants from distinct, eligible keys. A plan grant (3.5) releases a signed plan instead.

The execution receipt is the executor's signed statement that the receipt's call ran: it copies the decision id, decision hash, policy pin, target and request context from the receipt it honored, adds the hash of the outputs, the time, and, for a state-bound call, the state hashes before and after.

The evidence entry is one line in the executor's hash-chained log for each decision it took, honored or refused: the kind, the tool, the argument hash, the principal, the verdict code, the receipt, the grants or plan release, the execution receipt, the client that asked, and the target reference. Refusals are entries too: a policy refusal with no receipt, a declined hold with the receipt the human did not release.

The witness entry is the issuer's signed, hash-chained record of its own decisions: every receipt issued, every grant accepted, every decline, every plan, and every start of the service.

The plan is a signed, bounded scope: a list of steps, each a tool with exact arguments or argument patterns, the principal, the target, the policy pin, the approval terms, a note, an expiry. One grant releases the plan; each later matching call is released by it, step by step, each step once.

3.3 The chain

The objects chain on the receipt's decision id and decision hash. A grant names the decision hash and the hold; an execution receipt copies the decision id and hash; an evidence entry holds all three; the witness holds the issuance and the grant by the same ids. An auditor with the issuer's, approvers' and executor's public keys checks, for one call: that the receipt is well formed and issuer-signed and was unexpired when the call ran; that, if flagged, the grants release exactly it under its own terms; that the execution receipt is executor-signed and binds to it field for field, including the pre-state; and that the outputs, if supplied, hash as recorded. For a log: that every entry hashes and links, that every honored entry's chain verifies, that every refused entry's receipt, if any, has issuer provenance, and, with the witness, that the two records agree.

3.4 Invariants

  • Fail closed. Every undecidable path is a refusal with a code: missing key, malformed object, unknown version, unreachable issuer, policy exception. There is no unsigned path and no default allow.
  • Single use. A decision id, a grant id and a plan step are each claimed once in a replay store before the side effect, and the claim outlives the object's expiry plus clock skew.
  • Policy pin. A receipt carries the pin it was decided under; an executor compares it with the pin it holds live and refuses on any difference. Change the rules file and every outstanding receipt stops being honored, by construction rather than by revocation.
  • Exact binding. The live call may carry only the bound fields; any extra member is a refusal, so nothing can be smuggled past the check. Arguments bind by hash of their canonical form.
  • Separation of duties. Approver keys are never the issuer's; the check is by identity before signature, and the stronger guarantee is that the keys are generated in different places and never meet.
  • State binding. When the executor describes the target before asking, the receipt binds the description's hash; the approver sees the change against that state; the executor recomputes the hash before running and refuses if it moved.
  • Nothing asserted beyond proof. The audit labels a revert only when the state hashes form an exact round trip; names a plan release only when the recorded arguments re-match the step; marks a witness mismatch only by decision id. Where it cannot verify, it says so.

3.5 Plans: approving scope, not content

A card per held call is right for a mail rule and wrong for the twentieth cell edit of a reconciliation. A plan lets the assistant say what it intends as steps, lets the policy judge every step (a pattern step is judged with its patterns as the argument values, so a broad pattern fails a narrow rule), and lets a person approve the scope once. Each later flagged call that matches an unused step is released by the plan; the step is claimed once; the receipt is single-use as always; the evidence records the plan, the step and the arguments so an auditor can re-run the match. The trade is explicit: the approver sees tools, patterns, count, expiry and the note, not the contents. State binding still runs at the executor, but the human did not see the state. Which tools may be planned is therefore the policy's decision, and sends are kept individual by default.

3.6 Undo as another held write

Before a held, state-bound call runs, the executor keeps the target's bytes in a snapshot keyed by the state hash, with a sidecar naming the decision, the call, and, after the run, the state it left. An undo is a call the executor composes from the snapshot, through an adapter that knows the inverse verb: write the bytes back for a file; delete the object that appeared for a create; restore the writable fields for an update; re-create for a delete. It goes through the gate like any write: held, shown as a diff from the current content, bound to the current state, refused before any card if the target changed since, signed, executed once, recorded. The audit then recognizes the revert from the hashes alone, because the revert's pre-state is the original's post-state and its post-state the original's pre-state. No annotation is trusted, which is how the audit was able to decline a revert that was not exact (section 8.4).

4. The specification

The specification (SPEC.md, seventeen sections, about 940 lines) is the authoritative definition of every byte and every refusal; the implementations follow it, and a test fails if a refusal code exists in code that the specification does not name. This section summarizes what an implementer needs and why the choices were made.

4.1 Encodings

Every hash and signature is computed over the UTF-8 bytes of the RFC 8785 canonical form of an object: members sorted by UTF-16 code units, no whitespace, strings escaped only where JSON requires it, numbers as ECMAScript Number::toString. Gargoyle began with Python's default escaping and code-point ordering; it moved to RFC 8785 on 2026-10-18 after finding that the three receipt drafts of 2026 all use it, while the move cost only a format bump and a vector regeneration. The Python implementation is its own, including the ECMAScript number formatter, and reproduces the RFC's examples byte for byte; the TypeScript implementation emits object members by hand, because a sorted object through JSON.stringify moves integer-like member names to the front, which is not RFC 8785. That defect was found by the RFC's own key-ordering example in the test, before release. Signatures are Ed25519 (RFC 8032, pure) in lowercase hex; hashes are SHA-256 in lowercase hex; timestamps carry an explicit UTC offset or fail closed.

4.2 Verification order

An executor verifies a receipt in a fixed order, first failure wins: version; structure; provenance (a trusted issuer key is mandatory, there is no unsigned path); expiry; currency against the live policy pin; binding (principal first, then target, tool, arguments, state). Only then does it check the decision id, verify and claim the grants or plan release, and claim the decision id. The grants are checked and claimed before the receipt is claimed, so a refused grant leaves the receipt unspent. Every refusal has a name; Appendix B lists them.

4.3 Versions and historical verification

The receipt format is gargoyle/3; a verifier refuses other versions at admission. Evidence must outlive a format change, so the audit verifies earlier versions as written: a gargoyle/2 receipt with its grants and execution receipt under the canonical form it was signed with, and evidence entries of versions 1 to 4 under their own field sets. The vectors of the earlier format are frozen in the repository as a permanent test. When the format changed, the same fourteen-row log on the live workstation went from eight failures to none without a byte being rewritten.

4.4 Conformance vectors

Section 11 of the specification quotes, verbatim, the keys derived from named seed labels, a flagged receipt with delegation, its grant, its execution receipt, its JWS form, the exact hash-region and signed-region bytes, and a second vector with a two-approver threshold and two grants. The Python suite recomputes every value from the seeds and looks each up in the specification text; the TypeScript suite recomputes them independently and asserts byte equality. A reader who does not trust either implementation can recompute them with any RFC 8785 library and any Ed25519 library.

4.5 JWS form and identity input

A receipt may travel as a JWS in compact serialization with EdDSA, the signed receipt wrapped under a "gargoyle" claim with iss, jti, aud, exp and iat mirroring it, so that any JWT library can check the outer signature and the standard claims before a Gargoyle verifier checks the inner one. The same encoding, also under RS256 and ES256 with keys from a JWKS document, is the issuer's identity input: an Entra ID token's preferred_username is the principal, its groups and app roles are policy inputs, and delegation claims (RFC 8693 act, transaction-token agentic_ctx) become the on_behalf_of binding.

5. Identity: naming the person on both ends

A receipt that names a shared service account proves less than it seems to. The first live runs named one principal for every call on the workstation; the evidence could say what happened but not who asked. Both ends now carry a directory identity.

5.1 The requester

The workstation user signs in once with the Entra ID device-code flow (a `gargoyle login` command with MSAL as an optional dependency). The middleware's configuration switches from a shared token to that user's cached token, which MSAL refreshes silently before each call to the issuer until the tenant's policy demands a new sign-in; no usable token is a refusal, never a fallback to a shared credential. The issuer verifies the token against the tenant's published keys with the issuer app's client id as audience and takes the UPN as the principal. From then on every receipt, every card and every audit row names the person, rules can match their groups, and disabling the account in Entra stops the next call. Two bugs were found on the way and are recorded: MSAL inherited a private CA pin meant for the issuer, which required a per-request override of the trust store; and the issuer's audience check needed to accept a list so that init-minted and tenant tokens could coexist during migration.

5.2 The approver

An approver signs in with Microsoft in the console page itself, by OAuth code flow with PKCE written into the page in about forty lines, with no library; the RFC 7636 appendix vector is a test. The browser generates an Ed25519 key as non-extractable and keeps it in IndexedDB; there is no key file. On first use the page enrolls the public half under the approver's UPN; the issuer accepts the enrollment only from a token carrying the Approver app role (or a configured group) and takes the UPN from the token, never from the request. The issuer publishes the enrolled keys; the middleware fetches them when it checks a grant; the audit reads them from the deploy folder; threshold rules name people by UPN. The trust trade is stated in the specification: the issuer's operator thereby becomes the authority for who may approve, weaker than a root-signed key record and appropriate where one administrator runs both the service and the tenant. Separation of duties is unchanged: the issuer never sees a private key, and an enrolled key is never the issuer's.

5.3 The client

The receipt deliberately carries no model identity. The evidence entry does: the MCP client's name and version from its initialize handshake, so a fleet with Claude on some desks and ChatGPT on others has one trail that says who, through what, did what. The gate itself is model-agnostic by construction, and the handshake is also where it hands the model standing instructions (section 7.4).

6. Evidence: the log, the witness, and the folder

6.1 The evidence log

The executor appends one entry per decision to a JSON Lines file, each hashed over the one before. An honored entry carries the receipt, the grants or plan release, and the execution receipt; a refused entry carries what was asked and why not, with the flagged receipt when a human declined or let a hold lapse. The verifier recomputes each hash, checks each link, verifies every honored chain, checks every refused receipt's provenance, and reports per entry and for the whole file, with the tail hash. What the chain proves: no entry was edited, removed or inserted after the fact. What it does not prove: that the file is complete at its end, since truncation leaves a valid shorter chain; an auditor who records the tail hash at each audit detects it at the next. There is no mechanism for amending the log, because it is not amended.

6.2 The witness and reconciliation

The evidence log is the executor's own statement, and the executor is a workstation whose user administers the machine and holds the executor key. A dishonest workstation can forge an entry that verifies. So the issuer keeps a second record on the operator's server: one entry per decision it took, each hashed over the one before and signed with the issuer key, so a client can neither forge nor edit it, plus a started entry at every start so a restart is on record and the witness's horizon is known. Reconciliation verifies both chains and compares them by decision id. Four findings result, never resolved silently: evidence that honors a decision the issuer never issued or whose hash or principal differs; a held call honored without a grant the issuer saw for its hold or plan; a receipt the issuer issued to this principal that the client never recorded; an entry that predates its issuance. Evidence older than the witness's first start is reported as information, since the witness can vouch for it neither way. The limit is stated: the witness refutes a dishonest workstation, not a dishonest issuer; an operator who holds the issuer key can write a witness that agrees with forged evidence, and the controls for that are the controls for any signing key.

6.3 The folder

Every state-bound write leaves a post-state hash in the execution receipt and a target reference in the evidence entry. A tree check hashes every file under the gated folder that the evidence has a post-state for and compares; a file edited in Notepad, replaced by another tool, or deleted is named as changed outside the gate. Files the gate never touched are counted, not judged. No plaintext is needed. This is the side-door detector, and it is honest about what it is: detection after the fact, on a schedule, not prevention.

6.4 The audit command

One command verifies a log, reconciles it against a witness, and checks a folder, and prints per row: the kind, the tool, the person, the chain verdict, who released it, the plan and step if any, the state transition if any, the revert link if any, and the client. Findings print as lines an operator can grep, and the exit code is nonzero on any. The weekly review in the beta checklist is that command.

7. Implementation

7.1 Shape

About 7,700 lines of Python in 34 modules, four runtime dependencies (cryptography, FastAPI, uvicorn, requests), optional extras for Redis and for MSAL; a TypeScript port of the verifier, consumer and chain of about 730 lines; 407 Python tests and 9 TypeScript tests, run in CI on Python 3.12, 3.13 and 3.14. Released on PyPI as gargoyle-receipts through trusted publishing from tagged releases.

7.2 The middleware

The gate is an MCP server that fronts another MCP server. In stdio mode it runs beside the client as a local process and launches the upstream; in remote mode it is a Streamable HTTP server for hosted connectors and Microsoft 365 servers, with one shared upstream, sessions by header, a shared replay cache, evidence log and snapshot store, and a capability URL per person minted by the operator (the hosted client cannot carry the user's own token, so the operator vouches for the mapping, as with an API key, until OAuth replaces it). The middleware reads stdin and stdout as UTF-8 regardless of locale, a fix made after Windows decoded a client's UTF-8 as cp1252 and produced a lone surrogate the issuer could not serialize.

7.3 Adapters

What a held call changes is an adapter's business: it describes the target's state before and after, tells the approver what the call means, and composes the undo. The filesystem adapter reads a file's bytes. The Graph adapter fronts a Microsoft Graph MCP server by a JSON mapping of tool names to resource kinds (inbox rules, calendar events, contacts, Teams membership; sends, replies and posts marked irreversible), reads state through the upstream with the credential the write will use, summarizes the change in words with outside addresses called out, and undoes by the inverse Graph verb. The Graph adapter is tested against a fake server and has not run against a tenant.

7.4 Mandated, not named

The gate answers the MCP initialize handshake with standing instructions that clients place in the model's context for the session: every operation on the target goes through these tools, a wait is normal, a refusal is a decision to report rather than route around, plans and undo exist, the user never needs to name the server. With the client's other file tools disabled, there is one path and the model is told to take it. For a fleet, Claude Code's managed settings file, which the user cannot override, carries the MCP entry and the deny rules, so the gate is the administrator's mandate.

7.5 Hardening for a beta

The issuer's holds, grants, declines and plans live in SQLite so a restart drops nothing a person is acting on; a health endpoint reports the policy pin, pending counts, approver counts and whether holds are persistent; one JSON event per decision goes to the operator's log; init writes a systemd unit and an Apache virtual host; a checklist covers setup, what to tell users and approvers, what to watch weekly, and rollback.

8. Evaluation: a live deployment

The claims above were exercised on one deployment, built incrementally over two days: an issuer on an Ubuntu virtual machine with TLS from a private certificate authority; a Windows 11 workstation running Claude Code and then Claude Desktop; the project's own Python filesystem server behind the gate; Microsoft Entra ID for both identities; the approver's console in Edge. The workstation's evidence log reached forty-five rows, every one verified, and Appendix A lists them with the accounts replaced by their roles.

8.1 The gate works unnoticed, until it should not

Rows 1 to 11 are the first era: a shared principal, approval by a file-based key. Free listings and reads ran at once; a held write and a held rename were approved in the browser; a delete was refused by policy; and when the issuer was unreachable, the assistant itself declined to shell-write around the gate, which was welcome and is not relied upon. Row 4 is the write that timed out during an encoding incident, kept as a refusal with its flagged receipt.

8.2 Named identities

From row 12 the requester is the workstation's Entra account; from row 18 the releasing key is the approver's Entra account, enrolled from a browser-held key. Row 16 is the refusal that proved the enrollment path honest: the workstation still ran a version that trusted only its static approver key, so it refused a grant from the enrolled key with REF_APPROVAL_KEY_UNKNOWN, recorded it, and accepted the same grant after the upgrade.

8.3 Friction, measured

A crafted week-close task (read three files, render a review in the chat, write a report file and a reconciled task file, read both back) produced nine gated calls: seven free, two held, each released in eight to twelve seconds at a two-second poll. The cost of supervision for the whole task was two cards and about twenty seconds, and the approver saw the full content of both files before they existed. Rows 19 to 27.

8.4 State binding, undo, and the audit that would not lie

Row 29 is the first state-bound write: the audit reads "state b43c9319 -> 4de7ea40", the file as approved and as left. Rows 33 and 34 are a write and its attempted undo: the undo was held, released and state-bound correctly, its pre-state equal to the original's post-state, but the audit declined to print "reverts" because the restored bytes hashed differently. The cause was newline translation in the project's own filesystem server on Windows, which doubled every line ending on the way back. The server was fixed to read and write bytes exactly, and rows 36 and 37 are an exact round trip, "state ae799707 -> 0c8a2f3d reverts #36". The evidence caught a defect in its author's code on first contact, by refusing to assert what the hashes did not prove.

8.5 One card for a task

Rows 41 and 42: the assistant asked for a plan covering two held writes; one card; one grant; both writes ran with no further card; each row names the plan and its step, carries its own state transition, and names the client, "claude-ai 0.1.0", which is what Claude Desktop calls itself in the handshake. The issuer's log shows the plan hold's polls, one grant, then two receipts issued with no per-call hold, as designed.

8.6 The witness and the side door

Rows 43 to 45 were the first decisions after the witness began. Reconciliation reported them in agreement with the issuer's record, noted that the thirty-nine earlier rows predate the witness, and found nothing; the tree check verified both files the gate had ever written. Then one character of a gated file was changed in Notepad, and the next run reported "FINDING CHANGED_OUTSIDE_GATE" for that file, with the chain and the witness unchanged, because nothing had gone through the gate. That line is the whole point of section 6.3.

8.7 What the evaluation does not show

One deployment, one operator, one workstation, one approver, eleven days. Remote mode and the Graph adapter pass their tests and have not met a real client or a real tenant. The numbers are small and the identities are the author's own tenant. The evaluation establishes that the mechanism does what the specification says on real machines; it establishes nothing about operation at scale.

9. Threat model and limits

This section is the one a reader should weigh before any other. It says what the system is designed to resist, what it is not, and what would be needed to move each limit.

9.1 What the chain resists

  • A gate or issuer that approves its own holds. Refused by key identity before any signature check; the approver's key never exists on the issuer.
  • A receipt reused, altered, or presented for a different call. Single-use ids, exact binding by hash, principal check, refusal on any unbound member.
  • A policy changed under an outstanding receipt. The pin mismatch refuses it.
  • A target changed between the card and the run. State binding refuses it before the write.
  • An evidence entry edited, deleted or inserted after the fact. The chain breaks.
  • A workstation that forges a verifying entry. The witness has no such decision; reconciliation names it.
  • A held call honored without a release. The witness has no grant for its hold or plan; reconciliation names it.
  • A file changed by hand around the gate. The tree check names it on the next audit.
  • An assistant that is told a call failed and tries another way. The standing instructions say not to; the disabled built-in tools leave no other way through the client; a route through the shell is a side door, which the tree check catches after the fact.

9.2 What it does not resist, and says so

The workstation's own user. On a workstation the user administers, the gate constrains the assistant, not the user. The user holds the executor key, can edit the rules file (the pin changes, old receipts die, new ones follow the new rules), can edit the evidence (the chain breaks, and the witness disagrees), can replace the gate with a server of the same name, and can open Notepad. Each of these is detected by the audit, none is prevented. A workstation where the user is not the administrator, with the gate and its denials in managed settings, moves this limit; the tree check and the witness remain the controls where they are not.

The issuer's operator. A party holding the issuer key can forge receipts and a witness that agrees with them, and the audit cannot tell. The controls are those for any signing key: a hardware module, a separate host, rotation, and a second witness held by someone else, which is not built.

Truncation of the log. A log cut at an entry boundary is a valid shorter log. The witness catches a cut that drops honored decisions the issuer saw; an auditor who records the tail hash catches the rest.

The approver's judgment and screen. The approver signs the hash of the state and the change the executor reported; the executor's probe is what they saw. A probe lied to by its target, or an adapter that summarizes badly, is a bug in the adapter, not something a signature detects. The card shows the exact bytes for files, and words plus the probe for Graph objects, and a plan shows scope rather than content on purpose.

The rules themselves. The policy is the operator's; an open rules file is a correct deployment of a bad decision. The specification does not judge rules. It records which version decided.

Availability. An unreachable issuer is a refusal of every write. That is the intended failure mode and it has a cost: in one live run the assistant refused to work for twenty minutes while a certificate was fixed. Fail closed means that.

Clock. Expiries assume clocks within the stated skew; a workstation with a wrong clock refuses receipts or honors them late, in both cases visibly.

9.3 Limits of the evaluation

One author, one tenant, one approver, one workstation, eleven days, and no independent review that completed. The system verified its own claims on real machines and caught two of its author's bugs, which is more than Elyon-Sol managed in a year, and it is not an evaluation of the kind a reviewer would accept as sufficient. The evidence log of the deployment can be verified by anyone holding the three public keys; a copy with the accounts replaced is in Appendix A as text, and the live log stays on the workstation it belongs to.

11. What remains

  • OAuth on the remote gate. The capability URL is an API key; the hosted connector path should carry the person's own token through the OAuth 2.1 flow MCP defines, so remote mode names people the way stdio mode does.
  • Live runs of remote mode and the Graph adapter. Both pass their tests against fakes. The Graph run needs a server choice and its tenant permissions; the remote run needs a public endpoint and a hosted connector.
  • An outside reader. No one but the author has verified a chain. The specification, the vectors, and the verify-in-a-second-language test exist so that someone can; the work is not evaluated until someone does.
  • A key record for approvers. Enrollment makes the issuer's operator the authority for who may approve. A root-signed record, with the same windows and retirement the issuer key has, moves that authority to a party the operator does not control.
  • A second witness. The witness refutes a dishonest workstation and not a dishonest issuer. A second record held by a party who is neither closes that, and the reconciliation already compares two chains.
  • Multi-instance issuer. The hold store is one SQLite file; a second issuer instance needs a shared store and a single witness writer.
  • Adapters for the rest of the office. Excel, Word and the records that live in them have no adapter; the Graph mapping is the template, and each new kind of object needs a probe, a sentence and an inverse.
  • Draft alignment. Emitting and accepting the Acta, EP and XAIP shapes as described in the ally design, so a Gargoyle chain reads in those vocabularies.

12. Conclusion

A year of Elyon-Sol produced a gate that verified itself and that nobody asked for. Eleven days of Gargoyle produced a smaller thing with a sharper claim: for an assistant's tool call, a chain of signed objects that proves authorization, release, execution and state to a party who trusts no one in the loop, recorded on the workstation and witnessed off it, with its limits in the specification rather than in the small print. The evidence of the one deployment is forty-five rows, every one of which verifies, two of which the audit refused to flatter, and one of which is a human editing a file by hand and being caught. The lesson the predecessor taught is kept: the claim is only as good as the next outside reader, and the work is arranged so that reader needs three public keys and nothing else.
